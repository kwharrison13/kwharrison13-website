# Water in Catron County

**Full title:** *Water in the Biggest, Emptiest County: Quantities and Shortages in Catron County, New Mexico, 1880s–Present*
**Source:** Synthesized research report (principal sources listed at the end).
**One-line:** *A 140-year history of water scarcity and conflict in [Catron County](/notes/catron-county), New Mexico — the county [Luna](/notes/luna-new-mexico) sits in — from 1880s Anglo-Hispanic-Mormon settlement through the failed Gila diversion, the Augustin Plains "water grab," and into the 2000–present megadrought and 2026 drought emergency.*

> **Why this is here:** the **water** research strand of [The Luna Landing](/notes/the-luna-landing). Luna's viability has always turned on a narrow margin of diverted stream water; this report is the durable reference for the region's hydrology, water law, and drought trajectory. Reusable concepts mined out below.

## The shape of the problem

Catron County is a study in inverse proportion. It is the largest county in New Mexico by area — nearly 7,000 square miles, bigger than four U.S. states — and one of its emptiest, with roughly 3,500 to 3,800 residents. It is also, quietly, one of the most hydrologically complicated pieces of ground in the interior West. The Continental Divide runs through it, splitting the county between two great drainage systems: the Lower Colorado River Basin on the west (fed by the Gila, San Francisco, and Tularosa rivers) and the Rio Grande Basin on the east. Between and around them sit closed interior basins — most importantly the Plains of San Agustin — where whatever water falls stays put, never reaching an outlet stream.

That structure defines everything about the county's water story. Its perennial rivers rise in high, forested country (the Mogollon Mountains, the San Francisco range, the Gila high country) and carry snowmelt and baseflow down through narrow valleys where nearly all the arable land, and nearly all the people, are concentrated. Its groundwater sits in a patchwork of aquifers of very uneven productivity — some volcanic and sedimentary units yield only 1 to 15 gallons a minute, barely enough for a stock well. And its position at the far upstream end of the Gila system means that its surface water is legally capped from above by an interstate agreement the county had no part in negotiating. Water here has always been scarce, always contested, and — for the last quarter-century — measurably scarcer than at any point in twelve hundred years.

This report traces that story from Anglo-Hispanic settlement in the 1880s to the drought emergency of 2026.

## Before 1880: a long baseline of boom and bust

The people who settled the San Francisco and Tularosa valleys in the 1880s were not the first to test the region's water limits. The Mogollon culture — the makers of the celebrated Mimbres pottery — farmed these same highland valleys from roughly AD 1000 to 1130, then largely vanished from them. Tree-ring and climate reconstructions from the Luna and Reserve valleys covering roughly AD 680–1400 show pronounced swings between wet and dry regimes, and the broader Southwest endured a series of "megadroughts" between 800 and 1600 severe enough to be implicated in the abandonment of major Puebloan centers. The lesson embedded in the archaeology is one the county has relearned repeatedly: this is a landscape that can support settled agriculture in wet decades and then punish it in dry ones.

## The first water economy, 1880s–1910s

Three overlapping settlement waves arrived within a few years of one another, and each brought a different claim on water.

**Mining** came first and burned hottest. Silver and gold were found in the Mogollon Mountains in 1880 (the same year the prospector James Cooney was killed in an Apache raid), and the boomtowns of Mogollon and Cooney grew up around hard-rock mining that depended on water for placer washing and, later, milling and cyanide processing. The most visible artifact is the Catwalk near Whitewater Canyon, which today is a recreation trail but began as the pipeline-and-flume water system serving the mill town of Graham. Mining water demand was intense but geographically concentrated and, ultimately, temporary — most of these camps were spent by the early twentieth century.

**Hispanic settlement** along the San Francisco River predated the mining rush. Families had established plazas — the "Frisco" settlements that became San Francisco Plaza and, eventually, Reserve, the county seat — in the 1870s, farming valley bottomland with *acequias*, the gravity-fed community irrigation ditches that are the oldest European-derived water institutions in what is now the United States. The acequia model, brought from Spain by way of the Rio Grande valley, organized water as a shared community resource: a *mayordomo* (ditch boss) allocated flows, and every irrigator with rights owed annual labor on the *limpieza y saca*, the spring cleaning of the mother ditch. Under later New Mexico law these acequias became formal political subdivisions of the state, and several still operate on the San Francisco and its tributaries today.

**Mormon settlement** arrived in 1883, when the Swapp brothers (William, John, and Melvin) and other Latter-day Saints purchased a large land claim and founded the Luna Valley colony in the county's western reaches, near the Arizona line. The Luna settlers built a fort against raiders and cattle barons and, more durably, dug the Luna Irrigation Ditch to water the valley floor from Luna Creek and the upper San Francisco drainage — a system whose historic alignment was still being archaeologically documented a century later. Luna exemplifies the essential constraint of the whole county: viable farming was possible only where a reliable stream could be diverted onto a pocket of level, arable land, and those pockets were few and small.

**Ranching** quickly became, and remains, the dominant land use — and the one with the largest long-run effect on the water landscape. Cattle drives ran the Springerville-to-Magdalena trail by the mid-1880s, and open-range grazing spread across the vast public domain that would later become the Gila, Apache, and Cibola national forests. (The very name "Reserve" comes from the surrounding forest reserves.) Ranching did not consume much water directly, but by stripping perennial grass cover it set up a slow-motion hydrological transformation that would only become visible in later droughts.

## The ceiling from above: how Catron County's surface water got capped

An unusual feature of the county's water history is that the upper bound on its surface-water use was set not locally but in a interstate contest over the entire Colorado River system.

New Mexico sits at the headwaters of the Gila, but Arizona claimed the Gila's water downstream. In the landmark 1964 U.S. Supreme Court decree in *Arizona v. California*, the Court limited New Mexico's consumptive use of water from the Gila River, the San Francisco River, and San Simon Creek — capping how many acres the state could irrigate from designated reaches and how much water it could consume annually and over any ten-year period. In effect, the productive rivers of Catron County were legally rationed by a case the county was not a party to. The State Engineer completed a hydrographic survey of Gila Basin water uses in 1966, and those rights were subsequently sorted out in the long-running Gila River Adjudication. Locally, water was allocated under the doctrine of **prior appropriation** — "first in time, first in right" — meaning the oldest ditches and claims (many dating to the 1880s) hold seniority when the river runs short.

The practical upshot: even in a wet year, Catron County could not simply expand irrigation at will, and in a dry year the interstate ceiling compounded the natural shortage. This tension — abundant legal *entitlement* on paper, scarce *wet* water in the channel — runs straight through the modern disputes.

## The drought century: 1900s–1990s

Twentieth-century Catron County lived through the same droughts that shaped the rest of the arid Southwest, and its ranch economy magnified them.

The **1930s Dust Bowl** decade brought severe drought to already-overgrazed rangeland. But the event that still functions as the regional "drought of record" was the **1950s drought**, which gripped New Mexico and the Southern Plains from roughly 1950 through 1957. Its severity was compounded by policy: rapid water-supply development and generous permitting during the unusually wet 1940s had built dependence on flows that simply did not return, so the pain when supplies collapsed was worse than the rainfall deficit alone would suggest. For ranchers, drought meant a brutal binary — buy expensive supplemental feed or liquidate the herd — and many across the region sold out and never came back.

The deeper legacy was ecological. A century of habitual overgrazing, punctuated by the droughts of the 1930s and 1950s, tipped large areas of southwestern New Mexico grassland into shrub-dominated desert — a transition scientists at the nearby Jornada Experimental Range have tracked for over a hundred years and consider effectively irreversible on human timescales. Bare soil produced more blowing dust, and airborne dust in turn suppresses precipitation and accelerates snowmelt, a self-reinforcing feedback. The land's capacity to hold and slowly release water — the thing that keeps streams perennial through the dry season — was permanently diminished. The adaptive response, learned the hard way, was to run far fewer cattle than the range was once thought to support; surviving ranchers became, by necessity, careful land managers, and many invested heavily in wells and pipelines to move water to stock during dry spells.

## The modern water fights, part one: the Gila diversion that wasn't

The most expensive water controversy in the county's history grew out of an attempt to finally *use* that paper entitlement from the 1960s.

When Arizona needed New Mexico's support to settle Gila River Indian Community water claims, Congress passed the **Arizona Water Settlements Act (AWSA) of 2004**. Buried in it was a benefit for New Mexico: the right to develop up to an additional **14,000 acre-feet per year** (averaged over any ten years) of Gila and San Francisco water above the 1964 decree limits, together with a substantial federal subsidy — the crucial difference from earlier, unfunded dam proposals (Hooker, Connor, Mangas) that had never penciled out. The catch was structural: New Mexico did not own the water outright and would have to pay exchange fees to the Gila River Indian Community, which would in turn buy replacement Colorado River water.

In 2014 the New Mexico Interstate Stream Commission voted to pursue a diversion, and a quasi-governmental **New Mexico Central Arizona Project (CAP) Entity** was created in 2015 to build it. For the next six years the project foundered. Critics — led by Silver City conservationists, hydrologists, and the late writer-conservationist "Dutch" Salmon — argued that in most years the Gila simply had no surplus water to divert, that local irrigators weren't even using the rights they already held, and that the plan could never survive federal environmental review or make economic sense. Millions in federal dollars flowed to engineering firms and attorneys with no shovel ever hitting the ground. In **June 2020 the Interstate Stream Commission voted 7–2 to stop funding the required environmental impact statement**, effectively killing the diversion. The state's "New Mexico Unit Fund," which had accumulated to well over $100 million, was subsequently redirected toward smaller, practical water projects across southwest New Mexico — water-line and well repairs, treatment systems, and effluent reuse.

## The modern water fights, part two: the Augustin Plains water grab

The county's other signature water battle concerned groundwater, not rivers — and specifically the closed basin under the **Plains of San Agustin**.

Because it is a closed basin, the San Agustin aquifer is essentially a savings account with almost no deposits: it is not recharged by any perennial stream, so water pumped out is, for practical purposes, gone. Beginning in **2007**, Augustin Plains Ranch LLC — a property assembled by the Italian financier Bruno Modena — applied to pump **54,000 acre-feet per year** (about 17.6 billion gallons, nearly half of Albuquerque's annual use) from roughly three dozen wells and pipe it more than 150 miles north to the Albuquerque–Rio Rancho metro area. Local landowners around Datil and Magdalena, who depend on that same aquifer for their homes, gardens, and livestock, discovered the plan in a legal notice and organized to fight it.

The State Engineer rejected the application as **speculative** — the company could not identify who would actually buy the water or how it would be put to "beneficial use," the touchstone of New Mexico water law — in **2012**, rejected a revised version in **2014**, and the office rejected it yet again through the courts. In **April 2024**, a Seventh Judicial District Court judge, ruling in a packed courthouse in Reserve, granted summary judgment against the ranch for a third time. The case became a rallying point that united conservative ranchers, tribes, small-business owners, and conservationists, and it established an important precedent: rural groundwater cannot be monopolized for speculative export. As one Catron County commissioner put it, the recurring fear was that if it could happen on the San Agustin, it could happen anywhere in rural New Mexico.

## The megadrought and aridification, 2000–present

Underlying every recent dispute is a fundamental shift in the water baseline itself. Since roughly 2000, southwestern North America — Catron County squarely included — has been in what scientists now call an emerging **megadrought**. Using tree-ring reconstructions of soil moisture stretching back to AD 800, researchers found that **2000–2021 was the driest 22-year period in at least 1,200 years**, exceeded in the record only by a megadrought of the late 1500s. From 2000 to 2021, regional precipitation averaged about 8.3 percent below the 1950–1999 norm while temperatures ran about 0.91°C warmer, and roughly 40 percent of the soil-moisture deficit was attributable to human-caused warming.

The distinction matters: this is not just a run of dry years but **aridification** — a warmer atmosphere that pulls more moisture out of soil, snowpack, and streams even when precipitation is only modestly below average. Warmer winters mean less snowpack and earlier melt, which shortens the season during which the high country slowly releases water to the Gila, San Francisco, and Tularosa. The perennial reaches that made settlement possible in the 1880s are, in the long run, less reliably perennial.

## Today: the 2026 emergency and the fire–water nexus

The current conditions are the sharpest illustration of the trend. In **May 2026, Governor Michelle Lujan Grisham declared drought and severe fire conditions statewide amid what her office called the worst drought conditions on record**, urging counties to adopt water-conservation measures and burn restrictions; the state had recorded 366 wildfires in the first four months of the year, roughly double the same period in 2025.

Catron County sits at the center of this. As of mid-2026 the **Sacaton Fire** was burning uncontained across the Gila Wilderness, feeding on dead-and-down timber left by the **2012 Whitewater-Baldy Fire** — which at roughly 300,000 acres was the third-largest wildfire in New Mexico history — and the Gila National Forest was under fire restrictions attributed explicitly to long-term severe drought and mounting tree mortality.

Wildfire is where drought closes the loop on water *quantity and quality*. Severe fires on steep, forested headwaters strip the vegetation and soil structure that regulate runoff; the next rains then arrive as flash floods carrying ash, sediment, and debris into the very streams — the Gila, the San Francisco, Whitewater Creek — that supply the valleys below. A drought-driven fire can therefore degrade a watershed's water supply for years after the flames are out, silting diversions, fouling municipal and stock water, and scouring channels. In a county whose entire water economy depends on a handful of high-country streams, the fire regime supercharged by the megadrought is arguably the single largest emerging threat to water security.

Against that backdrop, a parallel policy fight continues over the **M.H. "Dutch" Salmon Greater Gila Wild and Scenic Rivers Act**, reintroduced in Congress in 2023 and again in 2025, which would designate roughly 450 miles of the Gila and San Francisco river system as Wild and Scenic. Supporters frame it as protection against future "dewatering" of New Mexico's last major free-flowing river; opponents, including some Catron County officials and the Heritage Waters Coalition, warn about impacts to existing diversions, water rights, and industry. It is, in essence, the 1880s question in modern dress: who gets to claim these scarce flows, and for what.

## Synthesis: the throughline

Across 140 years, a few durable patterns define water in Catron County:

- **Structural scarcity.** A huge county with tiny, stream-dependent pockets of usable water, split across two ocean-bound basins and several closed interior ones, has never had water to spare.
- **A ceiling set elsewhere.** Since 1964, the county's most productive surface water has been legally capped by an interstate decree, so "more water on paper" has repeatedly collided with "no wet water in the channel" — the story of both the acequia era and the failed Gila diversion.
- **The overgrazing ratchet.** Drought plus a century of heavy grazing converted grassland to desert, permanently lowering the land's ability to store and release water; the response has been steadily fewer cattle and more engineered water infrastructure.
- **Repeated defense against export.** From the Augustin Plains fight to the diversion debate, the recurring political act has been *keeping water in place* against schemes to move it — upstate, downstream, or out of basin.
- **A shifting baseline.** The 1950s "drought of record" has been eclipsed. The 2000–present megadrought is the driest stretch in more than a millennium, and warming-driven aridification and megafire are making the historic assumption of perennial mountain streams progressively less safe.

The settlers of Luna, Reserve, and the Frisco plazas built a life around the narrow margin between the region's wet decades and its dry ones. That margin is now thinner than at any time in the county's recorded — or reconstructed — history.
